# Executive summaries and decision documents

An executive summary lets the reader grasp the whole document on one page. Every reader reads it and many read little else, so it has to carry the full message.

## Anatomy

1. **Context** - situation and complication in two sentences.
2. **Recommendation** - the governing thought in one sentence, in bold.
3. **Summary statement** - the arguments in one sentence, if useful.
4. **Arguments, point by point** - one bullet per section of the document. Each bullet opens with a bold lead that mirrors the section's action title, followed by the key numbers.
5. **Decision needs** - for decision documents, the decisions in their own box: numbered, one line of detail each, with "why now" (the deadline that forces the decision).

## Best practices

1. **Bullets, not prose.** Bold the leads so the page can be read from the bold text alone.
2. **Mirror the document.** The summary follows the table of contents, so the reader can navigate from it.
3. **Follow the action titles.** If in doubt, the summary is the condensed sequence of the document's action titles.
4. **Quantify.** "EUR 15m a year against budget" convinces; "significant upside" does not.
5. **Answer first, content not process.** Not "we ran a tender and a consumer test", but what they showed.

## Decision needs

A senior document ends by saying what is asked of the reader. Not with a data page, and not with a generic add-on such as "...and decide today".

- **Which decisions** - each one a sentence that can be approved or rejected ("Approve the tender for outbound logistics"), not a topic to discuss.
- **By when** - the date the decision is needed.
- **Why now** - the event that forces that date.
- **Safeguard** - where useful, the answer to the main concern behind each decision.
- **Owner** - who carries it after approval.

Next steps follow the decisions: task, owner, date.

## Decision pre-reads

A pre-read for a decision meeting has to work without a presenter.

- **A format that works:** cover, one-page executive summary, up to four supporting pages. Agree on one page convention (the cover does not count) and refer to pages by name ("the savings page"), not by number.
- **The summary asks for the decisions; the last page repeats them,** each paired with its safeguard and an owner, plus the next steps.
- **Answer each stakeholder's main concern on the page before it is raised** - for example a small box explaining why the savings differ from a figure quoted earlier.
- **Align before the meeting.** Test the storyline in one-to-one conversations with the key decision makers. A concern raised in a one-to-one can be solved; a concern raised in the meeting gets defended.

## Forms by channel

The anatomy stays the same. Length and layout change.

### Email to leadership

```
Subject: [Decision needed by <date>] <governing thought in a few words>

<Context: one or two sentences.>

**Recommendation: <governing thought in one sentence>.**

- **<Lead mirroring section 1>** <key numbers>
- **<Lead mirroring section 2>** <key numbers>
- **<Lead mirroring section 3>** <key numbers>

Decisions needed by <date>:
1. <Decision>
2. <Decision>
Why now: <the forcing event>.

Next step: <task, owner, date>. The full deck is attached; the <named page> has the detail on <main concern>.
```

- The subject line carries the ask and the date. A reader who opens nothing else still knows what is wanted.
- No greeting paragraph about the project, no account of what the team did.
- Point to the attachment by page name, once, at the end.

### One-page summary or memo

The same five parts, with a heading per part or a decisions box at the foot. One page. If it runs over, a section is carrying evidence that belongs in the body.

### Spoken update

Three sentences and a close:

1. The governing thought ("Our current hypothesis is...").
2. "For three reasons": first, second, third, one clause each.
3. The next step, or the decision you need.

Process appears only in the close. If asked "what is the status?", the answer is the conclusion so far, not the activity so far.

### Slide

The executive summary page of a deck: the governing thought as the action title, context in one line, one numbered point per section with a bold lead, and the decisions box. The `mbb-deck` skill renders it.

## Condensing an existing document

- Take the governing thought and the arguments from the source. If you would state them differently, say so and ask; do not improve silently.
- One bullet per section of the source, in the source's order. A bullet with no section behind it is an addition, and the reader will look for it in vain.
- Carry numbers over exactly: same value, unit, baseline and period. Where the source gives two figures for the same thing, stop and raise it.
- Carry the decisions over exactly, with their deadline. If the source asks for none, say so: a summary cannot supply an ask the document does not make.
- Drop the appendix, the method and the caveats that do not change the decision.

## Briefing inputs

A summary is only as good as its brief. Before drafting, have these five; the user defines the first three.

1. **Role and audience** - who writes, for whom.
2. **Objective** - what the reader should do afterwards ("approval of three decisions on 12 May").
3. **Governing thought** - in one sentence.
4. **Structure** - for example opening, one bullet per argument, then the decisions.
5. **Facts and limits** - which sources to use, the baseline, a word or page limit, and the rule that no figure is invented and every missing figure is listed.

A brief without audience, governing thought and structure produces a summary of the project files, not a recommendation. Three things stay with the user throughout: the governing thought, the baseline, and the final check of every number.
