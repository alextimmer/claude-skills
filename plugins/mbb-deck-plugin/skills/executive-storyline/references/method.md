# The storyline method

The structural backbone of any executive communication: slides, memo, email or spoken answer.

## The pyramid principle

Top-down communication starts with the governing thought and follows it with the arguments that support it, each backed by evidence.

```
                 Governing thought
              (one sentence: the answer)
                        |
        ---------------------------------
        |               |               |
   Argument 1      Argument 2      Argument 3
        |               |               |
    Evidence        Evidence        Evidence
```

Work runs bottom-up: gather data, analyze, conclude. Communication runs top-down: conclusion first, then support. **Never present in the order you worked.**

The pyramid is the outline of the document. The governing thought and a summary of the arguments come first; then one section per argument, with its evidence. That holds for a deck and for a memo.

### Three rules

1. **Grouping.** A limited number of elements per level. Three is often ideal; more than five is too many. Group related arguments instead of listing them flat.
2. **Logic.** Elements on one level sit on the same logical level. A piece of evidence for an earlier argument is not a new argument; fold it under the argument it supports.
3. **Order.** Choose the order and be able to name it:
   - temporal (oldest to newest, or the reverse)
   - importance (most to least)
   - causal (cause to effect)
   - structural (abstract to tangible, or by a fixed breakdown such as region or function)

### MECE: the structure below the answer must hold

- **Mutually exclusive** - no overlaps. Each element belongs in exactly one place. Overlaps usually appear when one level mixes two dimensions, such as sections by product next to a section by region.
- **Collectively exhaustive** - no gaps. Together the elements cover the whole question. Gaps appear when an obvious member of the dimension is missing.
- **The test:** for each level, name the one dimension it uses (by product, by region, by driver, by phase). Check that every element follows it and that all members are covered.

### Overview and double-click

A document, and each chapter of a longer one, opens with an overview: the governing thought with one row per argument. Every section that follows is a double-click on one row: same order, same wording, same numbers. A reader who has seen the overview recognizes at once where a detail section belongs.

## Content beats process

Every statement is one of three things:

| Kind | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Governing thought | A recommendation or conclusion | "The plant should move to two shifts to hold its delivery promise." |
| Content | A supporting fact or argument | "Order intake rose 22% while output rose 4%." |
| Process | A report of activity | "We spoke to the plant managers and built a model." |

Focus on content. Process is not the message. At most it appears at the end, as the next step ("next, we validate the shift model with the works council").

When reviewing, classify every statement into these three. A draft dominated by process has no message.

## From data to "so what"

Every finding can be stated on four levels, and each answers a different question.

| Level | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Data | What are the numbers? | "Returns were 9% of orders last quarter." |
| 2. Summary | What happened? | "Returns doubled in a year, almost all of it in footwear." |
| 3. Insight | Why does it matter? | "Footwear now loses money on every second order, because sizing differs by supplier." |
| 4. So what | What should the reader do? | "Introduce one size chart across suppliers before the autumn range ships." |

Most drafts stop at level 1 or 2 and leave the rest to the reader. The standard is levels 3 and 4.

The right so what depends on the reader. The same data leads Finance, Operations and Marketing to different actions. Write it for the person who has to act.

Four tests for a governing thought:

- **Goal** - what is this work for?
- **CEO test** - what should senior management do differently after hearing it?
- **Relevance** - would the story suffer if this point were missing?
- **Insight** - is it merely interesting, or does it change a decision?

## Choosing the structure

**Top-down is the default.** Start with the governing thought, then the arguments. Readers who know the context want the answer first.

**Lead with a short narration only when needed:** the audience is new to the topic and needs the context, or the message is controversial and the audience must be brought along.

**The standard document framework combines both.** Open with situation, complication and resolution, in roughly 10 to 20% of the document; the resolution states the governing thought and previews the structure. Spend the remaining 80 to 90% on the arguments and their evidence, in pyramid order.

### The opening (SCQA)

- **Situation** - an uncontroversial statement about the status quo. The audience nods.
- **Complication** - what makes the situation worth attention now. It is not necessarily a problem.
- **Question** - the question the complication raises. Write it down in the working storyline even if the final document only implies it. The governing thought must answer exactly this question.
- **Answer** (also: resolution) - the governing thought, plus a preview of how the document substantiates it.

Six complication framings, each with its natural question:

| The complication is that... | The question becomes |
|---|---|
| something went wrong | What do we do? |
| something could go wrong | How do we prevent it? |
| something changed | What should we do? |
| something could change | How should we react? |
| there are different points of view | Who is right? |
| there are several alternatives | Which one do we take? |

A missing or artificial complication is why audiences fail to lean in: the document never says why this matters now.

## Numbers and claims

- **Define every target and figure:** what is measured, against which baseline, for which period, by when. "EUR 8m of annual savings by 2028 against the approved budget", not "EUR 8m of efficiency".
- **Use the reader's baseline and terms.** Finance tracks savings against the budget, not against a do-nothing case. A figure the decision maker cannot find in their own numbers costs credibility.
- **Attribute each effect to the right lever.** If two measures produce the result together, say so, instead of crediting one.
- **Claim only what the evidence proves.** If one of four criteria points the other way, write "on three of four criteria".
- **Keep numbers identical everywhere.** The same figure in the storyline, the summary, the pages and the appendix. Separate components explicitly ("EUR 6m below budget plus EUR 2m of avoided fees") instead of merging them into one headline number.
- **Name what changes when.** For transitions, say from which date the new terms apply and who delivers until then.

## Dot-dash format

Draft the storyline as a working artifact, not as prose:

- bullets for the messages (the governing thought and the arguments),
  - dashes for the supporting points under each message,
- at most one sub-level, and never a single dash on its own.

Send it to the manager or sponsor for alignment before any page is built.

### Worked example

Brief: a regional grocery chain asks whether to keep its own delivery fleet.

```
Audience:   Executive committee; CFO decides. Main concern: service level in rural areas.
Objective:  Approval of the tender in the committee meeting.
Question:   Should we keep operating our own delivery fleet?
Order:      importance

- Outsource delivery to a logistics partner from 2027, keeping own drivers in the rural routes
  - Cuts delivery cost per order while holding the service level
  - Three arguments: cost, service, feasibility
- Our own fleet costs more per order than a partner would
  - Cost per order against the three bids received
  - Fleet replacement falls due in 2027
- Service holds if the rural routes stay in-house
  - On-time rate by route type, own fleet against partner pilot
  - Rural routes are the only ones where the pilot fell short
- The switch is feasible within the current contract terms
  - Lease expiry dates by vehicle group
  - Works council agreement covers transfers
- Decisions needed: launch the tender; confirm the rural exception
  - Why now: lease renewals must be signed or declined by the end of the quarter
  - Next steps: tender documents, owner Head of Logistics; works council briefing, owner HR
```

The numbers are left as named placeholders because the brief gave none. Fill them only from the user's material.
